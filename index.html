I am an Assistant Professor of Business Administration at Truman State University. I received my Ph.D. in Finance from Texas A&M University in 2025.

My research studies how firms acquire innovation from outside their own boundaries, and what that acquisition does and does not transfer. Recent work examines corporate venture capital as a precursor to acquisition, the retention of inventors after a merger, and the acquisition of green technology by firms under environmental regulation. I work with patent text, deal-level data, and machine learning methods.

Job Market Paper

When Pressure to Be Green Becomes Demand for Green Credentials (with Kangryun Lee)

Abstract

A firm under environmental regulation is recorded twice. The regulator records whether a plant met the condition written into its permit, and the audience that evaluates the firm keeps a separate record that ranks firms and credits what the firm has in place. We ask whether firms under regulatory pressure respond by buying green technology that moves the audience's record and not the regulator's. We use the 2005 designation of fine particulate nonattainment counties under the Clean Air Act, measuring exposure from the share of a firm's pre-shock air emissions originating at plants in counties later designated, and we identify a green acquisition from the target's own granted green patents, so the label does not depend on the acquirer's stated motive or the target's industry. Exposed firms become 4.08 percentage points more likely to acquire a target holding green technology, against a base rate of 2.86 percent, and we find no rise in overall acquisition activity. The increase is concentrated in climate and clean energy technology, acquisitions of air-pollution abatement technology show no detectable response, and the increase is larger at firms that more analysts follow. Following the deals the firm's environmental rating rises, while we detect no reduction in emissions and no reorientation of the acquirer's own invention. The targets and the patents are real, so what we document is not a divergence between what a firm says and what it does. It is a divergence between what the firm holds and what the regulation was written to change.

Try Before You Buy: Measuring Innovation Synergy Using Textual Analysis

Abstract

An increasing number of acquirers make corporate venture capital (CVC) investments in private firms before acquiring them, a "two-step merger" strategy. Using textual analysis of patents within a real-options framework, I trace this strategy across three stages. Before the CVC, acquirers are more likely to invest in targets whose technology is more opaque, consistent with CVC as a way to gather information under uncertainty. After the CVC, pairs with greater technological alignment are more likely to proceed to an M&A. After the merger, acquirers that previously invested via CVC expand their technological scope, and patents filed by target and acquirer inventors share more newly aligned keywords and closer thematic similarity than in withdrawn deals. CVC thus serves as a real option that lays the groundwork for innovation synergy in later M&A.

Choosing Twice: What the Contract Does Not Transfer in the Acquisition of Innovation

Abstract

An acquisition of an innovative firm conveys patents under the purchase agreement and the inventors who produced them under no comparable obligation. A corporation that had invested in the target before bidding has observed it from inside and knows more about what it buys than an outside acquirer. Using this variation in corporate venture capital, the paper asks whether an acquirer's prior information shows in which target it selects and which of a target's inventors it retains. Across 29,022 inventors in 2,517 acquisitions of venture-backed firms from 1981 to 2022, acquirers with prior stakes select better targets, more highly cited and technologically more distant, and the pattern holds where no corporation held a stake, so it reflects the acquiring firm. Overall, these acquirers retain about as many of the target's inventors as other buyers, and the difference, 50.3 percent against 42.9, is not significant. The shortfall is confined to the top-ranked inventors who hold most of the technology, who are 14.9 percentage points less likely to be retained. Prior information improves which target an acquirer selects but not which of its inventors it keeps.

AI CAPEX and Innovation: Evidence from GPU Adoption (with Raymond Kim)

Abstract

Firms that buy AI computing capacity may substitute purchased methods for methods they would otherwise have invented. Using firm-level adoption of graphics processing units, we find that adoption reduces internal process patenting while total patenting, product patenting, research budgets, and inventor headcount are unchanged. The contraction is broad rather than confined to the methods purchased, and it operates at the extensive margin of adoption.

Who Mimics? Leaders, Followers, and the Decline of Capital Structure Peer Effects

Abstract

Extending Leary and Roberts (2014) to a 1975 to 2023 sample, this paper documents heterogeneity in capital-structure peer effects. Firms that lead their industry show no measurable response to peers while followers respond strongly, and the decline in the aggregate peer effect after 2000 is accounted for by the entry of firms that do not mimic rather than by a change among incumbents.
